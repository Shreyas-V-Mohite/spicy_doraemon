# Functional groups

A functional group is a specific arrangement of atoms within a molecule that is responsible for the molecule's characteristic chemical reactions. Molecules that share a functional group tend to react in similar ways, no matter how large the rest of the molecule is. Learning to recognise a handful of groups lets you predict how a new molecule will behave.

## Hydroxyl group and alcohols

A hydroxyl group is an oxygen atom bonded to a hydrogen atom (-OH) and attached to a saturated carbon. Compounds that contain this group are called alcohols, and ethanol is the most familiar example. The O-H bond is polar, so alcohols can form hydrogen bonds with each other and with water. This is why small alcohols mix easily with water and why alcohols have higher boiling points than hydrocarbons of similar size.

## Carboxylic acids

A carboxylic acid has a carbonyl group (C=O) and a hydroxyl group on the same carbon, written -COOH. Acetic acid, the acid in vinegar, is the simplest everyday example. Carboxylic acids are acidic because the hydrogen of the O-H group can leave as H+. After it leaves, the remaining carboxylate ion is stabilised by resonance: its negative charge is shared equally across both oxygen atoms instead of sitting on one.

## Why alcohols are weaker acids

An alcohol is a much weaker acid than a carboxylic acid. When an alcohol loses H+, the negative charge sits on a single oxygen atom with nowhere to spread. That ion is less stable, so the proton is held more tightly. The difference in acidity between ethanol and acetic acid is a factor of roughly a hundred billion, and resonance in the carboxylate ion is the main reason.

## Esters

An ester has a carbonyl carbon bonded to an oxygen atom that is itself bonded to another carbon, written -COO-R. Esters are formed from a carboxylic acid and an alcohol, with water released in the reaction. An ester has no acidic O-H hydrogen, so it does not behave like an acid. Many esters have pleasant fruity smells. Aspirin contains both an ester and a carboxylic acid, which makes it a useful molecule for practising how to tell the two groups apart.

## Amines

An amine contains a nitrogen atom bonded to carbon atoms and hydrogen atoms. Amines are classified as primary, secondary, or tertiary depending on how many carbon groups are attached to the nitrogen. The lone pair of electrons on nitrogen makes amines basic, so they can accept a proton. This is the opposite of a carboxylic acid, which gives one away.
