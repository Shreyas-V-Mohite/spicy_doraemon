# Lecture 7: Acidity and Molecular Structure

CHEM 2310, Organic Chemistry I. These notes go with the Lecture 7 slides. They cover the pKa scale, the four factors that control acid strength, resonance in alcohols, phenols and carboxylic acids, inductive effects, substituted benzoic acids, the basicity of amines, and the ionization of aspirin.

## The central idea

An acid is stronger when its conjugate base is more stable. Every comparison in this lecture comes back to one question: after the proton leaves, how well does the molecule hold the negative charge that is left behind? A conjugate base that spreads its charge out, or holds it on an atom that tolerates negative charge well, is low in energy, so the acid gives up its proton more readily.

## Reading the pKa scale

The pKa of an acid is the negative base-ten logarithm of its acid dissociation constant: pKa = −log Ka. A lower pKa means a stronger acid. Because the scale is logarithmic, each unit of pKa is a factor of ten in acid strength. Acetic acid (pKa 4.8) is about 10^11 times more acidic than ethanol (pKa 16), because the two pKa values differ by about eleven units.

Some reference values to remember: trifluoroacetic acid 0.2, acetic acid 4.8, phenol 10.0, water 15.7, ethanol 16, and the C–H next to the carbonyl group of acetone 19.

An acid–base equilibrium always favours the side with the weaker acid. Hydroxide ion is the conjugate base of water (pKa 15.7). Hydroxide therefore deprotonates acetic acid essentially completely, because acetic acid (pKa 4.8) is a far stronger acid than water. Hydroxide only partly deprotonates ethanol, because ethanol (pKa 16) and water (pKa 15.7) are almost equally acidic.

## The four factors: atom, resonance, induction, orbital

When comparing two acids, look at the conjugate bases and ask these four questions in order. The order matters because the earlier factors usually have the larger effect.

Atom: which atom carries the negative charge? Across a row of the periodic table, a more electronegative atom holds negative charge better, so acidity increases from CH4 (pKa about 50) to NH3 (about 38) to H2O (15.7). Down a group, a larger atom spreads the charge over a bigger volume, so H–I is a stronger acid than H–F.

Resonance: can the negative charge be delocalized over several atoms? Delocalization lowers the energy of the conjugate base. For O–H acids, resonance is usually the largest effect, and it explains why acetic acid (4.8) is so much stronger than ethanol (16).

Induction: are there electronegative atoms nearby? Electronegative atoms pull electron density through the sigma bonds and stabilize a nearby negative charge. The inductive effect falls off quickly with distance and is small after about three bonds.

Orbital: what kind of orbital holds the lone pair? An orbital with more s character holds electrons closer to the nucleus, which stabilizes the negative charge. The order is sp > sp2 > sp3, which is why ethyne (pKa 25) is far more acidic than ethane (pKa 50).

## Alcohols: ethanol

Ethanol, CH3CH2OH, has a pKa of 16, about the same as water. When ethanol loses its proton it forms ethoxide, CH3CH2O−. In ethoxide the negative charge sits on a single oxygen atom with no resonance to spread it out. The ethyl group donates a little electron density toward the oxygen, which slightly destabilizes the alkoxide.

Because ethanol is such a weak acid, ethoxide cannot be made in useful amounts with sodium hydroxide. It is prepared with sodium metal or with sodium hydride (NaH), whose conjugate acid, H2, is a far weaker acid than ethanol.

## Phenol: resonance into the ring

Phenol, C6H5OH, has a pKa of 10.0, so phenol is about 10^6 times more acidic than ethanol even though both are O–H acids. The difference is resonance. In the phenoxide ion, a lone pair on the oxygen is delocalized into the aromatic ring. Resonance places negative charge on the ortho and para carbons of the ring. Four resonance contributors share the charge, which stabilizes phenoxide far more than ethoxide.

Phenoxide is still less stable than a carboxylate. In only one of the phenoxide contributors does the charge sit on oxygen; in the other three it sits on carbon, which is less electronegative and holds negative charge less well.

## Carboxylic acids: acetic acid

Acetic acid, CH3COOH, has a pKa of 4.76. When acetic acid loses its proton it forms acetate, CH3COO−. In acetate the negative charge is shared equally by two oxygen atoms. The two resonance contributors are identical, so both carbon–oxygen bonds in acetate have the same length, 1.26 Å, which is between the length of a C–O single bond and a C=O double bond.

Carboxylate ions are especially stable because in both resonance contributors the charge sits on electronegative oxygen. This is why acetic acid is about 10^5 times more acidic than phenol and about 10^11 times more acidic than ethanol. The ranking carboxylic acid > phenol > alcohol is a direct result of how well each conjugate base delocalizes its charge.

## Inductive effects: halogenated acetic acids

Adding electronegative atoms near the carboxyl group makes the acid stronger. The pKa values are: acetic acid 4.76, chloroacetic acid 2.86, dichloroacetic acid 1.29, trichloroacetic acid 0.65, and trifluoroacetic acid 0.23.

Each halogen pulls electron density through the sigma bonds toward itself and away from the carboxylate, which spreads the negative charge and stabilizes the conjugate base. Fluorine is the most electronegative element, so trifluoroacetic acid, CF3COOH, is the strongest acid in the series: about 30 000 times stronger than acetic acid. The inductive effect weakens rapidly with distance. A chlorine on the carbon next to the carboxyl group has a large effect, while a chlorine three or four carbons away has almost none.

## Substituted benzoic acids

Benzoic acid, C6H5COOH, has a pKa of 4.20. Groups attached to the ring change its acidity. An electron-withdrawing group stabilizes the benzoate anion and makes the acid stronger; an electron-donating group destabilizes the anion and makes the acid weaker.

4-Nitrobenzoic acid has a pKa of 3.44. The nitro group withdraws electron density from the ring by both induction and resonance, which stabilizes the carboxylate, so 4-nitrobenzoic acid is about five times stronger than benzoic acid.

4-Methoxybenzoic acid has a pKa of 4.47. The methoxy group donates electron density into the ring by resonance through the oxygen lone pairs. This makes the carboxylate less stable, so 4-methoxybenzoic acid is a weaker acid than benzoic acid.

## Amines as bases: methylamine and aniline

The strength of a base is usually reported as the pKa of its conjugate acid, written pKaH. A higher pKaH means a stronger base, because the protonated form holds on to its proton more tightly.

Methylamine, CH3NH2, has a pKaH of 10.6. Aniline, C6H5NH2, has a pKaH of 4.6. Methylamine is therefore about 10^6 times more basic than aniline. In aniline the nitrogen lone pair is delocalized into the aromatic ring by resonance, so it is less available to accept a proton. Protonating aniline would destroy that resonance stabilization. In methylamine the lone pair is localized on nitrogen and is fully available to bond to a proton.

## Case study: aspirin and absorption

Aspirin, acetylsalicylic acid, is a carboxylic acid with a pKa of about 3.5. Whether aspirin is in its neutral form or its anionic form depends on the pH around it.

The Henderson–Hasselbalch equation connects pH and pKa: pH − pKa = log([A−]/[HA]). When the pH is one unit above the pKa, there is ten times more anion than neutral acid. When the pH is one unit below the pKa, there is ten times more neutral acid than anion.

In the stomach the pH is about 2, below the pKa of aspirin, so aspirin is about 97% in its neutral, un-ionized form. In the small intestine the pH is about 7, well above the pKa, so more than 99.9% of aspirin is present as the carboxylate anion.

Neutral molecules cross the lipid membranes of cells much more easily than ions. Because aspirin is mostly un-ionized at pH 2, it is absorbed well through the stomach lining. Ibuprofen is also a carboxylic acid (pKa about 4.4) and follows the same logic.

## Common mistakes

A strong acid has a weak, stable conjugate base, not a strong one. Students sometimes reverse this.

Resonance must involve the negative charge itself. Drawing resonance structures of the neutral acid does not explain acidity; compare the conjugate bases.

Inductive effects do not travel far. Do not expect a substituent five carbons away to change the pKa noticeably.

For bases, compare pKaH values, not pKa values of the neutral amines.

## Practice problems

1. Rank from most to least acidic: ethanol, phenol, trifluoroacetic acid, acetic acid. Answer: trifluoroacetic acid (0.23) > acetic acid (4.76) > phenol (10.0) > ethanol (16). Trifluoroacetic acid combines resonance with strong induction from three fluorines; acetic acid has resonance over two oxygens; phenol delocalizes the charge into the ring, mostly onto carbon; ethanol has no resonance at all.

2. Which is the stronger acid, benzoic acid or 4-nitrobenzoic acid? Answer: 4-nitrobenzoic acid, because the electron-withdrawing nitro group stabilizes the carboxylate conjugate base.

3. Would aspirin be absorbed better at pH 2 or at pH 7? Answer: at pH 2. Below its pKa of 3.5, aspirin is mostly neutral, and neutral molecules cross cell membranes far more easily than ions.
