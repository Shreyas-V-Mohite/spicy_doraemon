# Introduction to organic chemistry

Organic chemistry is the study of carbon-containing compounds. Carbon forms four bonds and bonds readily to itself, so it can build chains, branches and rings of almost any size. Most of the molecules of living things, and most medicines, plastics and fuels, are organic compounds.

## Functional groups

A functional group is a specific arrangement of atoms within a molecule that is responsible for the molecule's characteristic chemical reactions. Molecules that share a functional group react in similar ways, no matter how large the rest of the molecule is. Learning to recognise a handful of groups lets you predict how a new molecule will behave. The groups in this course are alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids, esters and amines.

## Naming organic compounds

Systematic names are built from a stem that counts the carbons in the longest chain: meth for one, eth for two, prop for three, but for four, pent for five, hex for six. A suffix then names the main functional group: ol for an alcohol, al for an aldehyde, one for a ketone, oic acid for a carboxylic acid. So a three-carbon alcohol is propanol, and a two-carbon carboxylic acid is ethanoic acid, better known as acetic acid.

## Alcohols and hydrogen bonding

An alcohol contains a hydroxyl group, an oxygen bonded to a hydrogen, attached to a saturated carbon. Ethanol is the best-known example. The O-H bond is polar, so the hydrogen carries a partial positive charge and the oxygen a partial negative charge. This lets alcohol molecules form hydrogen bonds with each other and with water. Hydrogen bonds are why alcohols boil at much higher temperatures than alkanes of similar size, and why small alcohols such as methanol and ethanol mix freely with water. As the carbon chain gets longer, the nonpolar part dominates and solubility in water falls.

## Aldehydes and ketones

Both groups contain a carbonyl, a carbon double-bonded to oxygen. In an aldehyde the carbonyl carbon is bonded to at least one hydrogen, so it sits at the end of a carbon chain. In a ketone the carbonyl carbon is bonded to two other carbons, so it sits in the middle of the chain. Formaldehyde and acetaldehyde are aldehydes; acetone is the simplest ketone. Aldehydes are easily oxidised to carboxylic acids, while ketones resist oxidation. The carbonyl carbon is slightly positive, which makes it a target for nucleophiles.

## Carboxylic acids and acidity

A carboxylic acid has a carbonyl and a hydroxyl on the same carbon, written -COOH. Acetic acid, the acid in vinegar, is the simplest everyday example. Carboxylic acids are acidic because the hydrogen of the O-H group can leave as H+. After it leaves, the remaining carboxylate ion is stabilised by resonance: its negative charge is shared equally across both oxygen atoms instead of sitting on one.

## Resonance

Resonance describes a structure that cannot be drawn correctly with a single Lewis structure. The real molecule is a hybrid of several drawings that differ only in where the electrons are placed. Spreading charge over several atoms lowers the energy of a molecule or ion, which makes it more stable. The carboxylate ion and the benzene ring are the standard examples. Resonance structures are drawn with a double-headed arrow between them; the molecule does not flip between them.

## Why alcohols are weaker acids

An alcohol is a much weaker acid than a carboxylic acid. When an alcohol loses H+, the negative charge sits on a single oxygen atom with nowhere to spread. That ion is less stable, so the proton is held more tightly. The difference in acidity between ethanol and acetic acid is a factor of roughly a hundred billion, and resonance in the carboxylate ion is the main reason.

## Esters

An ester has a carbonyl carbon bonded to an oxygen atom that is itself bonded to another carbon, written -COO-R. Esters are formed from a carboxylic acid and an alcohol, with water released in the reaction; this reaction is called esterification. An ester has no acidic O-H hydrogen, so it does not behave like an acid. Many esters have pleasant fruity smells and are used as flavourings. Aspirin contains both an ester and a carboxylic acid, which makes it a useful molecule for practising how to tell the two groups apart.

## Amines and basicity

An amine contains a nitrogen atom bonded to carbon atoms and hydrogen atoms. Amines are classed as primary, secondary or tertiary depending on how many carbon groups are attached to the nitrogen: one, two or three. The lone pair of electrons on nitrogen makes amines basic, so they can accept a proton. This is the opposite of a carboxylic acid, which gives one away. Small amines often have strong fishy smells.

## Isomers

Isomers are different molecules with the same molecular formula. Structural isomers differ in how their atoms are connected: butane and isobutane both have four carbons and ten hydrogens but are bonded differently, and ethanol and dimethyl ether both have the formula C2H6O but have different functional groups and very different properties. Stereoisomers have the same connections but a different arrangement in space.
